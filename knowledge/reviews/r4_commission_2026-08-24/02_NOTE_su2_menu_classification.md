## Abstract

Up to conjugation by the identity component of the automorphism group, the Lie algebra so(4) contains exactly three three-dimensional subalgebras: its two chiral ideals g₊ and g₋, and the class of graphs Δ (equivalently, the stabilizers of unit vectors in ℝ⁴). Up to the full automorphism group, g₊ and g₋ are one class. On the half-spinor modules S⁺ ⊕ S⁻ of Spin(4), a chiral ideal acts as su(2) on one summand and annihilates the other, while Δ acts at full strength on both; restricted to a single summand, a chiral ideal and Δ generate the same operator algebra. Consequently, given the premise that weak isospin is a three-dimensional subalgebra of so(4) acting on the internal side of the spinor module, the single empirical datum that right-handed fermions are weak-isospin singlets refutes Δ and forces the chiral ideal, uniquely up to an orientation convention. Nothing here derives the premise, supplies gauge dynamics, or fixes the orientation.

## 1. Setup and conventions

Let (V, δ) be ℝ⁴ with the standard Euclidean inner product and orthonormal basis e₁,…,e₄. Identify so(4) ≅ Λ²V as usual (X_{e_i∧e_j} = E_{ji} − E_{ij}). The Hodge star ⋆ : Λ²V → Λ²V satisfies ⋆² = id and depends on a choice of orientation; its ±1 eigenspaces

> g₊ := Λ²₊V,  g₋ := Λ²₋V,  dim g₊ = dim g₋ = 3,

are ideals: so(4) = g₊ ⊕ g₋ as a direct sum of Lie algebras, each factor isomorphic to su(2). (Basis: F±ᵢ = ½(e_i∧e₄ ± ½ε_{ijk} e_j∧e_k) up to normalization; [F±ᵢ, F±ⱼ] = ∓ε_{ijk}F±ₖ in one common convention — the two ideals carry structure constants of opposite sign when both are written in the Hodge eigenbasis relative to a single orientation. Nothing below depends on this sign convention except Remark 3.4, where it is the point.)

Spin(4) = SU(2)₊ × SU(2)₋ acts on the half-spinor modules S⁺ and S⁻ (each ≅ ℂ²): g₊ acts on S⁺ by the defining representation of su(2) and on S⁻ by zero; g₋ symmetrically. The chirality (volume) element acts as ±1 on S±; every element of so(4) commutes with it, so **every** subalgebra of so(4) preserves the Weyl grading — no candidate on the menu below mixes chiralities.

For a unit vector v ∈ V, stab(v) := {X ∈ so(4) : Xv = 0} ≅ so(3).

## 2. Two lemmas on su(2)

**Lemma 2.1 (subalgebras).** Every proper nonzero subalgebra of su(2) is one-dimensional. In particular su(2) has no two-dimensional subalgebra.

*Proof.* Equip su(2) with its (negative-definite) Killing form; in an ε-orthonormal basis the bracket is the vector cross product on ℝ³, so [x, y] ⊥ x and [x, y] ⊥ y. If h were a two-dimensional subalgebra with basis {x, y}, then [x, y] ∈ h ∩ h^⊥ = 0, so h would be abelian; but for the cross product x × y = 0 forces x ∥ y, contradicting independence. ∎

**Lemma 2.2 (automorphisms).** Aut(su(2)) = Inn(su(2)) ≅ SO(3).

*Proof.* An automorphism preserves the Killing form, hence lies in O(3) in the basis above; preserving the cross product forces determinant +1 (the cross product is orientation-sensitive: φ(x×y) = det(φ) · φ(x)×φ(y) for φ ∈ O(3)). So Aut ⊆ SO(3) = Inn ⊆ Aut. ∎

## 3. The classification

**Theorem 3.1.** Up to conjugation by Inn(so(4)) = SO(3) × SO(3), the three-dimensional subalgebras of so(4) = g₊ ⊕ g₋ are exactly: (i) g₊; (ii) g₋; (iii) the graph class Δ = {Γ_φ := {(a, φ(a)) : a ∈ g₊} : φ an isomorphism g₊ → g₋}, which is a single conjugacy class.

*Proof.* Let h ⊆ g₊ ⊕ g₋ with dim h = 3, and let π± be the two projections. Write k₊ := h ∩ g₊ = ker(π₋|_h) and k₋ := h ∩ g₋ = ker(π₊|_h), so that

> dim h = dim k₊ + dim π₋(h) = dim k₋ + dim π₊(h).

π±(h) is a subalgebra of g± ≅ su(2), hence of dimension 0, 1, or 3 (Lemma 2.1).

Case π₋(h) = 0: h ⊆ g₊ with dim 3, so h = g₊. Symmetrically h = g₋ when π₊(h) = 0.

Case dim π₋(h) = 1: then dim k₊ = 2, i.e., g₊ contains a two-dimensional subalgebra — impossible (Lemma 2.1). Symmetrically dim π₊(h) = 1 is impossible.

Case dim π₊(h) = dim π₋(h) = 3: then dim k₊ = dim k₋ = 0, so both restrictions of π± to h are bijective and h = Γ_φ with φ := π₋ ∘ (π₊|_h)⁻¹ : g₊ → g₋ a Lie algebra isomorphism. Conversely every Γ_φ is a subalgebra. Two graphs Γ_φ, Γ_ψ are conjugate under (α, β) ∈ Inn(g₊) × Inn(g₋) iff ψ = β ∘ φ ∘ α⁻¹; since Aut(su(2)) = Inn (Lemma 2.2), any two isomorphisms are so related, and Δ is one class. ∎

**Corollary 3.2 (up to the full automorphism group).** Out(so(4)) ≅ ℤ₂ is generated by the swap of the two ideals, realized by Ad_r for any orientation-reversing r ∈ O(4) (since r reverses orientation, Ad_r ⋆ Ad_r⁻¹ = −⋆, exchanging g₊ ↔ g₋ and fixing Δ setwise). Hence up to Aut(so(4)) the menu has **two** entries: the chiral class {g₊ ∼ g₋} and Δ. "Assigning g₋ rather than g₊" is the same subalgebra under the opposite orientation convention; it becomes a distinct branch only if some independent structure pins the orientation.

**Proposition 3.3 (Δ = the vector stabilizers).** For every unit v ∈ V, stab(v) ∈ Δ, and Δ = {stab(v) : |v| = 1} as a conjugacy class.

*Proof.* Take v = e₄; stab(e₄) = span{e_i∧e_j : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 3}. Each e_i∧e_j decomposes as ½(1+⋆)(e_i∧e_j) + ½(1−⋆)(e_i∧e_j) with both components nonzero (⋆(e₁∧e₂) = e₃∧e₄ ∉ span{e_i∧e_j}_{i,j≤3}), so both projections π±(stab(e₄)) are nonzero subalgebras of su(2) of dimension ≥ 1; since stab(e₄) is simple (≅ so(3)) its projections have no kernel unless trivial, forcing dim π± = 3 and stab(e₄) = Γ_φ for some φ. Conversely SO(4) acts transitively on unit vectors, so the stabilizers form one conjugacy class, which by the above is Δ. ∎

**Remark 3.4 (non-examples, stated precisely).** (a) The "boost-like" triple span{e₁∧e₄, e₂∧e₄, e₃∧e₄} is not a subalgebra: [e₁∧e₄, e₂∧e₄] = −e₁∧e₂ (up to convention), outside the span. (b) For a linear (not necessarily Lie) isomorphism φ : g₊ → g₋ and α ∈ (0, π/2), the "mixture" {cos α · a + sin α · φ(a)} is a subalgebra iff tan α · φ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, i.e., iff the mixture is itself a graph — a Δ-class member. In the Hodge eigenbasis, where the two ideals carry opposite structure-constant signs, the naive identity-matching a_i ↦ b_i is an **anti**-homomorphism, so equal-weight "handed" mixtures built on it fail to close for every α; the unique closing mixture matches a_i ↦ −b_i and lands in Δ. There are no chiral–chiral hybrids.

## 4. The discriminator on the spinor module

**Theorem 4.1.** Restrictions to the half-spinor summands:

| host | action on S⁺ | action on S⁻ |
|---|---|---|
| g₊ | defining su(2) (image dim 3) | **0** (singlets) |
| g₋ | 0 | defining su(2) |
| Δ (any Γ_φ) | defining su(2) | defining su(2), full strength |

Moreover, restricted to S⁺ alone, g₊ and any Δ-member generate the **same** operator algebra (both restrict onto the full su(2) of traceless anti-Hermitian operators on S⁺); they are indistinguishable by any probe confined to one Weyl summand.

*Proof.* Immediate from the Spin(4) module structure of §1: g₊ acts by the defining rep on S⁺ and by 0 on S⁻; a graph Γ_φ = {(a, φ(a))} acts on S⁺ through its g₊-component a and on S⁻ through φ(a), each ranging over the full su(2). ∎

**Remark 4.2.** The discriminator is therefore located entirely in S⁻: for the chiral host S⁻ is an exact weak-singlet sector; for Δ it is a second doublet sector coupled at the same strength as the first. The discriminator is empty unless S⁻ is occupied by physical states; the selection below consumes that occupancy as a named structural fact.

## 5. The conditional selection

**Premise H (hosting; endorsed, not derived).** Weak isospin is realized as a three-dimensional su(2) subalgebra of so(4), acting on the internal (body-frame) side of the local-state/spinor module.

**Datum D (read from experiment; reversible).** Right-handed fermions are weak-isospin singlets: no right-handed charged current is observed at any accessible energy.

**Occupancy O (structural; consumed).** S⁻ is occupied — in the parent programme, the charged lepton occupies both Weyl summands (its Dirac-mass channel) — so D has something to bite on.

**Theorem 5.1 (selection).** Assume H, D, O. Then the weak host is the chiral ideal, uniquely up to Aut(so(4)) — i.e., uniquely up to the orientation convention of Corollary 3.2.

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1 the candidates are g₊, g₋, Δ. By Theorem 4.1, any Δ-member makes the occupied S⁻ a full-strength second doublet sector, contradicting D. By Corollary 3.2, g₊ and g₋ are one assignment under relabeling. ∎

**Corollary 5.2 (hypercharge constancy).** Any generator Y commuting with the host acts as a scalar on each irreducible host-doublet (Schur), so hypercharge is constant across weak doublets.

**Corollary 5.3 (chirality of the coupling).** g₊|_{S⁻} = 0 is precisely the statement that the weak current is built from one chirality: at the level of currents and quantum numbers this is the V−A structure, generation-blind because the host acts identically on every copy of the doublet module.

## 6. Side placement (why "internal side" in H is not a free convention)

Work in Cl(4, 0) ⊇ so(4) (as grade 2). Left- and right-multiplication operators commute identically by associativity: L_a R_b x = a(xb) = (ax)b = R_b L_a x. So an internal (right-side) su(2) automatically commutes with the observer's Lorentz generators (left side); its label is a Lorentz scalar. Conversely, on the observer's side after the lock, the centralizer of the boost set {e₁, e₂, e₃} in Cl(4, 0) is span{1, e₁₂₃} with e₁₂₃² = −1 — two commuting real dimensions, isomorphic to ℂ. Two commuting directions contain no su(2); no observer-side host exists. (Direct computation: an element commuting with e₁, e₂, e₃ commutes with each generator; checking grades, only 1 and e₁₂₃ survive.)
